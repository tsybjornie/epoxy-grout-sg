# Negotiation — The Playbook

`SALES-SCRIPT.md` is persuasion: moving quality from invisible to verifiable.
This document is the layer under it — what to do when the other side is not
merely uncertain but *negotiating*: making moves, testing the price, playing
you against competitors, or buying at volume. Same rule as everything else in
this folder: every mechanism named, every claim one we can act on.

---

## 0. The strategic posture, stated once

Our entire negotiation strategy is **pre-commitment** (Schelling, *The
Strategy of Conflict*): we published the tariff, so we have visibly removed
our own ability to move on rate. This looks like giving up flexibility. It is
the opposite — in bargaining, **the party that cannot move wins the haggle**,
because there is nothing to haggle over. Every move below is about defending
that commitment without losing the customer.

Three consequences, internalise them:

1. **A discount is not a concession — it is a breach.** The moment one
   customer gets 10% off, the published tariff becomes a *starting price*,
   and every future conversation is a negotiation. The asset is destroyed
   retroactively for everyone who paid list.
2. **All flexibility lives in scope, schedule and payment structure** —
   dimensions where giving something costs us less than it is worth to them
   (the definition of integrative trade, Raiffa).
3. **We are playing the repeated game; the customer is playing a one-shot.**
   (MATH.md §6, STRATEGY.md §5.3.) We can accept losing any single deal.
   That asymmetry is our real leverage, and it only works if we genuinely
   walk when the deal goes below floor.

## 1. Know both BATNAs before replying (Fisher & Ury)

**Ours:** the next enquiry. Demand arrives continuously from the site; a
crew-day given to a haggled-down job is a crew-day taken from a list-price
job. Our reservation price is therefore not "anything above material cost" —
it is **list minus the value of a free crew-day**, which is roughly list.
This is why we can be relaxed. Be relaxed.

**Theirs, honestly assessed:**

| Their alternative | Real cost to them | What it means for us |
|---|---|---|
| Do nothing | $110/yr bleed + escalation risk ($600 job → $3,500 job once water passes the membrane) | Time is on our side. Never pressure; state the physics once and stop |
| Cheap regrout ($150–300) | Winner's-curse quality (MATH.md §6.5): skim removal, urethane, 2-yr warranty that expires before the evidence arrives | Don't attack it — hand them the three questions (SALES-SCRIPT.md) |
| Re-tile ($3,500) | The anchor. Nobody's actual plan | Mention only as the avoided alternative |
| DIY epoxy | Pot life + mixing ratio make this genuinely hard; failed DIY costs more to fix than to do | Respect it; offer the honest warning, not scorn |

A customer with a *strong* BATNA (new BTO, joints open, ten firms would love
the job) should be treated differently from one with a *weak* one (mould
returning for the third time, wedding photos in six weeks). Not in price —
in **pace and patience**. Price discrimination by desperation is exactly what
the published tariff exists to make impossible; that promise is worth more
than any single capture.

## 2. The ZOPA is in dimensions, not dollars

The zone of possible agreement on *rate* is deliberately zero-width. The real
negotiation space:

| Dimension | We can give | It costs us | It is worth to them |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Scope** | Second bathroom / kitchen added at the marginal (declining) rate — AC′(A) < 0 is real arithmetic, MATH.md §4.2 | Nothing — it is the honest price | A visible "deal" |
| **Schedule** | A date when we are already in their area (mobilisation attribution, MATH.md §8.3) | Genuinely less | Feels like a favour |
| **Payment** | Small deposit, balance strictly on completion + photo pack | Cash-flow timing | Removes their performance risk — the thing they actually fear |
| **Evidence** | Extra documentation: joint-depth photos, batch numbers, dated stages | Minutes | Their insurance against us |
| **Colour/finish** | Time with the swatches, /v2/ visualiser session | Minutes | Ownership (IKEA effect) |

**Always trade, never give.** Every concession is exchanged for something —
a booking today, the second room, a review after 30 days, flexibility on
date. A concession given free signals the price was soft all along
(and invites the next probe). This is the single most common error.

## 3. MESO — offer three structures, same arithmetic

When a conversation stalls on price, do not defend the number. Offer
**multiple equivalent simultaneous offers** (Bazerman & Neale) — all priced
off the same tariff, differing in shape:

> "Three ways to do this, same arithmetic behind all of them:
> A — whole home now, $1,488, one mobilisation, colour matched throughout.
> B — master bathroom now at $488, common bath at tariff whenever you're
> ready; costs a bit more in total because it's two visits.
> C — bathroom + kitchen while I'm there, $1,010 on the calculator.
> Which shape fits how you live?"

What this does: moves the decision from *whether* to *which* (autonomy
preserved, reactance down); reveals their true constraint (cash-flow vs
disruption vs urgency — whichever option they push against tells you); and
demonstrates the tariff is a formula, not a posture, because all three
reconcile to it.

## 4. Live-chat move handling

The WhatsApp opening and the three questions are in `SALES-SCRIPT.md`. These
are the *negotiation* moves that come after.

**The probe ("best price?", "got discount ah?")** — often not a demand,
just Singapore. Answer the cultural question, hold the commitment:
> "Price is the published one — same formula for everyone, that's the whole
> point of putting it online. What I *can* do is scope: if the second
> bathroom goes in the same visit the rate band drops for both, and that's
> real money, not a discount."

**The competitor lever ("XYZ quoted $700 for the same thing").** Never match,
never rubbish. Decompose:
> "Might be a fair price — depends what's inside it. Ask them the three
> questions and line the answers up against ours. If they're removing to
> depth, using a named two-part epoxy, and warranting five years, take it —
> genuinely. Usually one of the three is the gap."
This converts a price comparison into an attribute comparison — the axis on
which we win (STRATEGY.md §4). If they still choose the cheap quote, that
was a customer buying on the one axis we don't compete on. Let them go well:
they come back at year two, and they remember who told them the truth.

**The split-the-difference reflex ("meet me at $1,300 lah").** The trap in
splitting is that it validates their anchor, not the price. Decline the
mechanism, not the person:
> "If I could take $1,300 I'd have published $1,300 — the number isn't a
> starting position, it's the formula's output. What moves the total is
> what's in the job. Want me to see if anything in the scope is doing less
> work than it costs?"
Then genuinely look — sometimes a line item *is* removable (a bedroom floor
they added out of completeness). Scope-trimming to their budget is honest;
rate-cutting is not. **"Trim the scope, never the rate."**

**The stall as tactic (versus the genuine "let me think").** The genuine one
gets the escalation physics, no deadline (SALES-SCRIPT.md). The tactical one
— silence engineered to pull a better offer — gets exactly nothing, which is
the point: chasing a stall with an improved offer teaches that stalling
works. One follow-up at 48–72h with *new information* (their itemised BOQ as
a PDF, a relevant finished-job photo), never a sweetener.

**The add-on squeeze (job done, "can you also do the kitchen strip, free
lah").** The relationship answer, not the marginal-cost answer:
> "Can't do it free — but while the gear's here it prices at the no-mobilisation
> rate, which is the cheapest that strip will ever be: $X."
Post-completion is also when the *aggregation* moment peaks (they've seen
the finish): the moment to mention the review, not to defend $80 of labour.

**The genuine hardship case.** Distinguish from the probe. If real, the
lever is payment structure and scope phasing — never rate: master bath now,
common bath next quarter, both at tariff. We lose nothing; they get a path.

## 5. B2B — contractors, IDs, agencies (a different game)

Consumers are one-shots; a renovation contractor is a **repeated game with
volume**, and here — only here — structured pricing moves are legitimate,
because they are printed bands, not haggling outcomes:

- **Volume bands, published like everything else**: N jobs/quarter → the
  band rate. A schedule anyone can read is a tariff, not a discount.
- **Their real currencies differ**: program reliability (we show up when the
  schedule says), clean handover photos for *their* client file, and
  someone to blame safely. Price these in; they're worth more than margin.
- **Payment terms are the risk**: consumer pays on completion; a GC on
  30-day terms is an unsecured loan. Terms price into the band —
  net-on-completion gets the best band, net-30 does not.
- **The white-label ask** ("quote it under our name") is fine; the
  **warranty passthrough** is not negotiable — the certificate carries our
  name and batch numbers regardless, because the homeowner is the one
  holding it at year four.

## 6. When to walk, and how

The floor is not a mood; it is written down: minimum job $380, tariff rate,
warranty terms, removal depth, named product. Below any of those lines the
job is a loss or a liability. Walk warmly and leave the door open —

> "I don't think I'm the right fit for this one price-wise. If it changes,
> or if the cheap fix doesn't hold, the calculator's always there — and the
> three questions go with you whoever does it."

The walk-away is the commitment device made flesh. One haggled exception
leaks (Singapore is small, and renovation forums are smaller); the reputation
for *never* moving is what lets every future conversation skip the haggle
entirely. That is the asset. Guard it.
