# Warranty Length — The Full Decision

"Why not just offer a short warranty?" is a fair question and I under-argued the other
side. Here is the whole thing: the genuine case for a short warranty, every branch each
party can take, and where the reasoning actually lands.

---

## 1. The real case FOR a short warranty

Five arguments, and three of them are good.

**1.1 It's cheaper, obviously.** Total cost (reserve plus non-covered call-outs) is **$6
at two years against $40 at five** on a $1,074 bathroom. Real money on thin jobs.

**1.2 Our failure estimates are guesses.** The Weibull parameters η=25, m=2 were assumed,
not fitted from claims we've actually had — because we haven't had any yet. **If the true
failure rate is three times my estimate, a 5-year term costs $90 rather than $30, and a
10-year term costs $342.** A short term caps the damage from being wrong about our own
model. This is the strongest argument on this side.

**1.3 It matches the market, so nothing needs explaining.** Every competitor found offers
two years. Matching costs no sales conversation.

**1.4 Cash flow.** Reserves are money you hold and don't spend.

**1.5 A promise you can't back isn't a signal — it's a liability.** This is the argument
I completely missed, and it's the serious one. A warranty is only worth the firm behind
it. If a customer thinks we survive each year with probability *s*, the cover they can
rationally expect to collect is:

| Promise | s = 0.99 | s = 0.95 | s = 0.90 | s = 0.85 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 yr | 98% | 91% | 83% | 75% |
| 5 yr | 96% | 82% | **68%** | 55% |
| 7 yr | 95% | 77% | 59% | 45% |
| 10 yr | 93% | 70% | **49%** | 34% |
| 15 yr | 90% | 61% | 37% | 23% |

In effective years:

| Promise | at s = 0.95 | at s = 0.90 |
|---|---|---|
| 5 yr | 4.5 yr | 4.1 yr |
| 10 yr | 8.3 yr | 6.9 yr |
| 15 yr | 11.2 yr | 8.6 yr |

**A 15-year promise from a firm with 90% annual survival delivers about 37% of its face
value.** Anyone paying attention discounts it — so an unbacked long warranty can signal
*less* credibility, not more. It reads as either desperate or unconsidered.

---

## 2. The case AGAINST a short warranty

**2.1 Two years separates nothing (EQ 6.2).** A signal only works if it is cheap for a
good operator and expensive for a bad one. Grout failures are bimodal: **workmanship
faults surface at 12–36 months**, wear-out much later. A two-year term stops just as the
evidence arrives — which is precisely why every competitor offers exactly two. It costs a
bad operator almost nothing, so it carries no information.

**2.2 The break-even is very low.** How much extra business must a longer term win to pay
for itself, on a $1,074 job at 47% gross?

| Term | Total cost | Extra vs 2 yr | Needs conversion lift of |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 yr | $11 | $6 | +1.2% |
| **5 yr** | **$40** | **$25** | **+5.0%** |
| 7 yr | $76 | $53 | +10.6% |
| 10 yr | $150 | $109 | +21.7% |
| 15 yr | $290 | $229 | +45.3% |

**Five years pays for itself if it wins 5% more bookings than two years.** In a market
where nobody publishes price, nobody names their product, and only one competitor states
a warranty at all, a 5% lift is a low bar. Ten years needing 21.7% is a much harder one.

**2.3 One referral outweighs the whole thing.** A referral worth 30% of a job's profit is
**$151** — more than three times the entire 5-year warranty cost of $40. A warranty that
generates referrals is self-financing, and that is the actual commercial argument, not
the risk transfer.

## 3. Every branch — what each party can do

### 3.1 Our strategy space

`n ∈ {0, 1, 2, 5, 7, 10}` years, plus three modifiers that are separable choices:
**transferable or not**, **insurance-backed or self-backed**, **published exclusions or
buried ones**.

### 3.2 Customer, before buying

| # | Action | Our best response |
|---|---|---|
| A1 | Buys from us | — |
| A2 | Buys from a cheaper competitor | Nothing. Price shoppers on big floors were never ours |
| A3 | Asks us to match a cheaper price | Hold. Point at the tariff and the term difference. **Never discount the warranty to save the price** |
| A4 | Asks us to extend the warranty | Price it — $53 buys 7 years from 5. A paid extension is a fine product |
| A5 | Defers, does nothing | Cheapest re-marketing target we have. The cost-of-delay curve is the argument |
| A6 | Does it themselves | Let them. They come back in eighteen months |
| A7 | Collects more quotes | **Good for us.** Give them the comparison checklist — product name, removal depth, warranty term, GST |

### 3.3 Customer, after buying — with the cost of each

| # | Branch | Cost | What handles it |
|---|---|---|---|
| B1 | Nothing goes wrong | $0 | — |
| B2 | Genuine defect, reported promptly | **$644** | Fix it. This is what the reserve is for |
| B3 | Genuine defect, reported **late** | **$967** | Larger scope once water is behind it. This is why the terms say tell us early |
| B4 | Not covered, accepts the explanation | $80 | Explain in writing, quote separately |
| B5 | Not covered, disputes it | $160 | **Published exclusions are the defence** |
| B6 | Misuse (acid, abrasive), claims anyway | $160 | The damage pattern is distinctive. The care note is the defence |
| B7 | Tile or substrate failed, blamed on grout | $160 | **The Stage-0 photographs are the defence.** This is exactly why we take them |
| B8 | Sells the flat, transfers cover | $0 | Free — hazard is unchanged. Gains goodwill and a new contact |
| B9 | **Refers someone** | **−$151** | The branch that pays for everything else |
| B10 | Posts a bad review instead of calling | $240+ | **Worst branch.** Mitigate by making claiming trivially easy — one WhatsApp photo |
| B11 | Fraudulent or exaggerated claim | $160 | Batch records and photos settle it. Rare |

**The insight in that table:** B10 is worse than B2. A customer who complains publicly
instead of claiming costs more than one who claims. **So the design goal is to make
claiming easier than complaining** — which is why the claim route is a WhatsApp photo and
a 5-working-day promise, not a form.

### 3.4 After the term expires

| # | Branch | Response |
|---|---|---|
| C1 | Comes back for another room | The whole point |
| C2 | Refers | The whole point |
| C3 | **Fails just after expiry** | Judgement call. A goodwill repair at month 62 costs $644 and buys a review; refusing saves $644 and risks B10. Decide case by case, but lean toward fixing |

### 3.5 Competitor

| # | Branch | Response |
|---|---|---|
| D1 | Ignores it | Nothing needed |
| D2 | **Matches the term** | Fine — they now carry the same reserve without our removal-depth discipline. Compete on the *evidence*: named product, batch numbers, photographed joint depth |
| D3 | Undercuts on price | Expected on large floors. Hold the tariff |
| D4 | **"They won't be around in 5 years"** | The only genuinely dangerous attack, and §1.5 says it lands. Answer it with backing, not with a longer number |

## 4. Where this actually lands

**Keep 5 years standard.** It clears the 12–36 month failure window that a 2-year term
misses, it pays for itself on a 5% conversion lift, and at $40 all-in it is cheap enough
that being wrong about the failure model doesn't hurt much.

**Keep 7 years on whole-home.** Bigger job, better margin, and the extra $36 is
proportionate.

**Do not go to 10 or 15.** Not because of cost — 10 is affordable — but because of §1.5:
we cannot credibly back a decade yet, the customer discounts it to about seven effective
years anyway, and it needs a 21.7% conversion lift to break even. **A number we can't
back is worth less than a shorter number we obviously can.**

**The real upgrade is not length, it is backing.** What makes a warranty credible is
evidence it will be honoured:

- Batch numbers and dated photographs on every certificate — already doing this
- A named product with a receipt — already doing this
- **Third-party or insurance-backed warranty** — the actual answer to D4, and worth
  investigating. It converts "trust us for five years" into "here is who pays if we
  don't"
- Trading history, visibly stated, growing each year for free

**Revisit once there is claims data.** Every probability here rests on assumed Weibull
parameters. After ~30 jobs and any claims at all, refit them. If real failures are rarer
than assumed, 10 years becomes cheap and the calculus changes.

---

## 5. What would change the answer

| If this turns out to be true | Then |
|---|---|
| Failure rate is 3× my estimate | 5 yr costs $90 not $30. Still fine. **10 yr would cost $342 — the reason not to commit to it now** |
| Warranty length demonstrably drives conversion | Revisit 10 yr. It only needs a 21.7% lift |
| We can get insurance backing | Take it. Backing beats length |
| A competitor goes to 5 or 7 years | Our differentiator moves to evidence — batch numbers, photographed removal depth, named product. **Which is the more defensible ground anyway** |
| Claims cluster on one failure mode | Fix the method, not the warranty |
