# The Sales Conversation

Built on what the model actually supports. Every claim in here is one we can back —
because a script that outruns the evidence gets caught, and getting caught in this trade
costs more than the sale.

---

## The strategic position first

Before any words: **understand what game you are in.**

The customer cannot tell a good grouting job from a bad one at handover. Both look
identical. The difference shows up at 12–36 months, long after payment. That is textbook
**information asymmetry** (Akerlof), and it has a predictable consequence: if buyers
can't distinguish quality, they rationally pay only the average price, good operators
can't earn a premium, and quality drifts to the floor. **That is exactly why this market
is full of cheap regrouts that fail.**

You do not win that game by claiming to be good. Everybody claims to be good. **You win
by making quality checkable before payment** — which is what the published tariff, the
named product, the batch numbers and the photographed joint depth are all for. They are
**costly signals** (Spence): cheap for you to send *because you actually do the work*,
expensive for anyone who doesn't.

So the entire conversation has one job: **move quality from invisible to verifiable.**

## The spoken version

Roughly two minutes. Say it like a person.

> "Before I quote you anything — can I ask what made you look into this? Is it going
> black in the corners, or is it coming out?"
>
> *[Let them answer. Actually listen.]*
>
> "Right. So here's the thing most people don't get told, and it's not really your
> fault — cement grout is **porous by design**. That's not a defect, that's what it is.
> It drinks water, soap, body oils. When you scrub the black off, you're scrubbing the
> surface of something that's growing *inside* the joint. That's why it comes back."
>
> "You've basically got two options and they're priced very differently, but not in the
> way people expect."
>
> "You can re-do it in cement. Around three hundred dollars, and you'll be doing it
> again in about three years. Or epoxy — which isn't porous at all, it's a cured
> plastic — and that's more up front, but it lasts fifteen years plus."
>
> "So people ask me which is cheaper. **Cement works out at about a hundred and ten
> dollars a year. Epoxy is about forty-seven.** The epoxy is less than half the price —
> it just doesn't feel that way because you pay it all at once."
>
> "It pays for itself against cement in **five years**. After that it's free."
>
> *[Pause. Let that land. Don't fill the silence.]*
>
> "Now — you should get other quotes. I mean that. But can I tell you the three
> questions to ask, because they'll save you more than any discount I could give you?"
>
> *[They'll say yes. Nobody says no to this.]*
>
> "One: **what product are you using, and can I see the receipt?** Not 'epoxy' — the
> actual brand and name. Because real epoxy is two or three parts mixed on site against
> a clock. If it comes ready-mixed out of a tub, that's urethane, and it's not the same
> thing. Most people won't answer that question."
>
> "Two: **how deep are you removing the old grout, and will you photograph it?** It
> should be at least two-thirds of the depth. If they skim over the top, the new grout
> keys into the old cement instead of the tile — and it'll come off in sheets in about a
> year. You can't see it once it's finished. That's why the photo matters."
>
> "Three: **how long is the warranty?** Most of this trade says two years. Grout that
> was done badly usually fails between one and three years — so a two-year warranty
> stops right about when the evidence turns up. Ours is five, and seven for a whole
> home, and it transfers if you sell."
>
> "Ask us those three questions too. That's the point — I'm not asking you to trust me,
> I'm giving you a way to check."
>
> "My price is on the website, all of it. There's a calculator — you put your rooms in
> and it gives you a fixed number, itemised, before you talk to anybody. Same
> arithmetic for everyone. I can't quietly charge you more than the next person, because
> the formula's published."
>
> "Want me to work it out with you now, or would you rather look at it yourself first?"

## What each move is doing

| Move | Mechanism | Why it's honest |
|---|---|---|
| Opening with a question, not a pitch | Lowers reactance; you learn their actual problem | You genuinely need the answer to quote |
| "It's not really your fault" | Removes the defensiveness that blocks listening | It isn't their fault |
| Porous **by design** | **Reframing** — moves it from "your grout failed" to "this material always does that" | Straightforwardly true |
| $110/yr vs $47/yr | **Reference-point shift** (Kahneman & Tversky). The comparison changes from *price* to *cost per year*, where we win | Real arithmetic, EQ 5.1, and we show the working |
| "Pays for itself in five years" | **Loss aversion** — recasts the cement route as a recurring loss, not a saving | 5.1 years, computed |
| The pause | Silence does the persuading. Filling it undoes the work | — |
| "You should get other quotes" | **Reciprocity** + it's disarming. Nobody selling you something says this | We want them comparing on the right axis |
| The three questions | **Costly signal** (Spence) — cheap for us, expensive for anyone cutting corners. Also arms them against the **winner's curse** | Every question is one we answer well |
| "Ask us those three too" | Converts a sales claim into a verifiable test | The whole strategy in one line |
| Published tariff | **Credible commitment** (Schelling) — removing your own ability to move the price is what makes it trustworthy | It is genuinely published |
| "Same arithmetic for everyone" | **Fairness** — perceived fairness drives acceptance more than price level does (ultimatum-game result) | True by construction |
| Closing with a choice of *how*, not *whether* | Preserves autonomy, which raises follow-through | They can genuinely pick either |

## Handling the objections

### "It's too expensive"

Never defend the price. **Change the unit.**

> "Compared to cement, yes — about a hundred and eighty more today. But cement is a
> hundred and ten a year and this is forty-seven. If you're staying more than five
> years, epoxy is the cheaper option. If you're selling next year, honestly, cement
> might be the right call — I'd rather tell you that than sell you something you don't
> need."

Offering the cheaper answer when it's genuinely right is the single most effective thing
in this entire document. It costs one small job and buys the reputation that generates
referrals — and a referral is worth more than the job you declined.

### "I got a quote for half that"

Don't attack them. **Explain the structure.**

> "That might be a perfectly fair price — it depends what's in it. Ask them the three
> questions. Usually the gap is one of three things: they're not removing the old grout
> to depth, it's pre-mixed urethane rather than real epoxy, or the site fee and GST come
> later. Any of those is legitimate if you *know* about it. The problem is when you
> don't."

Worth knowing, and worth saying if it fits: **the cheapest of five quotes is, on
average, the one that most underestimated the job.** That's the winner's curse, and the
only ways out of it are all quality reductions.

### "Can you do better on price?"

You have a published tariff. **That is the whole point — it can't move.** But you can
trade.

> "I can't move the rate — it's published, and if I discount for you I'd be
> overcharging the person who paid it yesterday. What I *can* do is look at the scope.
> If you're doing the second bathroom too, the volume band drops the rate for both. And
> if you're flexible on dates I can fit you into a day I'm already in your area."

**Never discount the warranty or the material to hit a price.** Material is 6–12% of the
invoice — there is nothing to save there, and it's the one thing holding up the guarantee.

### "Let me think about it"

Accept it. Don't manufacture urgency.

> "Of course. One thing worth knowing while you decide — the cost doesn't stay flat. If
> it's just discoloured, it's a grouting job. Once water gets past it into the
> membrane, it's a bathroom. That's roughly six hundred versus three and a half
> thousand. I'm not saying that to rush you, and it doesn't happen overnight, but it
> only moves one direction."

That is a real hazard, stated honestly, with no deadline attached. **Never invent a
deadline.** Fake urgency is the most detectable lie in sales and it destroys everything
the rest of this conversation built.

### "How do I know you'll still be around in five years?"

The one attack that genuinely lands. **Don't bluff it.**

> "Fair question, and you shouldn't just take my word for it. What I can give you is
> the certificate with the batch numbers and dated photos of every stage, including the
> joints opened up before I grouted them. That's your evidence regardless of what
> happens to me — any competent contractor can read it and see the job was done
> properly."

Do not answer this with a longer warranty. A number you can't back is worth less than a
shorter one you obviously can.

## The WhatsApp version

Most enquiries arrive as a message. Same strategy, ninety percent shorter.

> Hi — thanks for getting in touch.
>
> Quickest way: our calculator gives you a fixed itemised price in about 30 seconds, no
> form first → cleangrout.sg
>
> If you're comparing quotes, three questions worth asking anyone (including us):
> 1. Which product exactly — brand and name, not just "epoxy"?
> 2. How deep is the old grout removed, and will you photograph it?
> 3. How long is the warranty? Most of the trade does 2 years; we do 5.
>
> Send me a close-up photo of a joint in daylight and I'll tell you what you've got and
> what it'd cost. No charge either way.

That last line is the highest-converting sentence available to you. It gives real value
before asking for anything, it starts a conversation that isn't about price, and it costs
a minute.

## Lines never to use

Each of these is either false, unsupportable, or destroys trust faster than it builds it.

| Never say | Why |
|---|---|
| "Anti-mould certified" | ISO 22196 is an *antibacterial* test and excludes building materials. The honest version — epoxy is non-absorbent so mould has nothing to feed on — is a better argument anyway |
| "Code requires epoxy" | No code anywhere mandates it. FDA and USDA explicitly decline to endorse products |
| "We'll match your tile colour exactly" | On neutrals we get close; on colour we can't, and epoxy can't be tinted on site. Promise "close on neutrals, deliberate contrast otherwise" |
| "Offer ends Friday" | Fabricated urgency. The most detectable lie in sales |
| "It'll be mouldy in six months" | Exaggerated fear. The real timeline is honest enough |
| "Lifetime warranty" | Uncostable, unbackable, and a 15-year promise already delivers only ~37% of face value from a small firm |
| "HACCP certified grout" | Doesn't exist. EC 852/2004 regulates food businesses, not products |
| Anything bad about a named competitor | Attacks read as weakness. Give the customer the questions and let the answers speak |

## The one-sentence version

If you remember nothing else:

> **Don't ask them to trust you — hand them the three questions and let the answers do
> the work.**

Everything above is machinery for that sentence. In a market where nobody publishes a
price, nobody names their product and nobody photographs what they buried, being the
firm that can be checked is the whole advantage — and it is one no competitor can copy
without actually doing the work.
